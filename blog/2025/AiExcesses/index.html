Artificial Intelligence (AI) excesses: implications for market valuation and the macroeconomy

Created on August 26, 2025

2025   ·   macroeconomy   AI   ·   articles

I. Background

The figure, from the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (via FRED), shows the share of U.S. gross domestic product (GDP) going to corporate profits after tax (with inventory valuation and capital consumption adjustments, IVA & CCAdj). The data spans from the early Q1:1947 to Q1:2025.

Note some patterns:

1) Post-WWII era (1950s–1980s): profits were relatively stable, generally between 4% and 8% of GDP, dipping during recessions. 2) 1990s–2000s: profits started trending higher, peaking around the dot-com boom (late 1990s) and again just before the 2007–09 financial crisis. 3) 2014 – 15 fall in profit share is partly related to the shale energy investment cutback, although it did not technically bring about a recession. 4 ) 2020 – 2025 : corporate profits reached historically high shares, peaking above 11% of GDP, well above the long-term postwar average.

Note that in the past peaks in profit shares often occurred shortly before recessions, visible in the late 1960s, late 1990s, mid-2000s, and to a certain extent before the 2020 COVID-19 downturn.

II. What could be the mechanism that brings a high corporate profit share of GDP into an economy recession, as happened in these instances in the past?

When corporate profits reach an unusually large share of GDP, it usually marks the late stage of the business cycle: margins are strong, investment is high, and confidence is widespread. However, if those investments don’t generate enough new revenue, rising depreciation charges and financing costs start to erode earnings. As profits come under pressure, firms scale back capital spending and hiring. Because business investment and payrolls make up a big part of overall demand, these cutbacks ripple through the economy and can ultimately push it into recession. We saw this mechanism in 1999– 2000, when extraordinary profits from the tech/telecom boom collapsed once over-investment in internet infrastructure became clear. Similarly, in 2006, record profit shares tied to housing and finance gave way to a bust that transmitted through lower investment and tighter credit. Can we be in a similar position today? Let me argue below that this could be the case for the AI sector. Here are the steps.

1) AI companies’ valuations are many multiples of their Net Profits

For example, Nvidia’s market cap is around $3.5 trillion, while its most recent annual net income was $ 60 - 65 billion. That gives a P/E ratio in the 50 - 60 × range, far above the “value” benchmarks of 5–10× profits that would correspond to a $0.3–0.7 trillion valuation. Microsoft at ~$3.5 trillion and ~$10 0 billion net income trades near 35 × profits, and Apple at ~$3 trillion and ~$9 5 billion net income trades around 30 ×. These figures confirm that AI leaders are valued at many multiples of their current earnings power, reflecting investor confidence in future profit expansion. The sheer gap between actual profit-based valuations and market prices demonstrates that today’s multiples are not grounded in current cash flows but in expectations of extraordinary growth.

2) AI companies are investing massively and at an unsustainable rate

In 2025, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Microsoft together are on track to spend more than $3 0 0 billion globally on capex, mostly on AI and data-center build-outs. Given that about half of capacity is U.S.-based, this implies more than $1 50 billion in U.S. AI-related investment, equal to roughly 0.6% of GDP and about 4% of total private nonresidential fixed investment. That’s nearly triple the share these firms represented in 2019, showing how sharply spending has accelerated. Within BEA’s national accounts, investment in servers jumped 41% year-on-year and data centers now account for about one-third of all office construction spending. Such rapid concentration of investment into one sector, at a pace far above trend, suggests the risk of overshooting near-term demand — a classic hallmark of unsustainable over-investment phases.

3) High valuations play a direct role in making these massive investments feasible

When a company trades at 30–50× profits, its cost of equity capital is effectively very low. Issuing new stock (or using stock as currency for acquisitions or compensation) dilutes existing shareholders less. Even if a company doesn’t raise new money by selling shares, having a high stock market valuation signals investor confidence. That confidence lets managers reinvest existing profits back into the business aggressively, because shareholders expect those investments to sustain growth and don’t punish the stock by lowering its valuation multiple.

Highly valued firms also enjoy tighter credit spreads and cheaper borrowing. For example, Microsoft, Apple, and Alphabet all carry top-tier credit ratings (AA/AAA). Their debt costs are close to U.S. Treasury yields, allowing them to borrow tens of billions at modest interest. Investors view the firms’ balance sheets and market positions as safe precisely because of their valuation and cash flow strength.

4) But financial risk is rising. If the $300B+ annual capex quickly turns into depreciation without commensurate AI revenue, earnings leverage compresses

With AI/data-center capex by the big four (Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft) already exceeding $300 billion annually, the flow of new servers and facilities will soon translate into very large depreciation charges on their income statements. Unless AI- related revenues and margins scale up as fast as depreciation, the apparent profit surge of 2023–25 could flatten or reverse. This is the essence of earnings leverage compression: fixed costs rise mechanically as assets age, but income gains are uncertain. In past episodes of sectoral over-investment, profits stopped rising and started to decline. The same risk now looms for AI leaders if usage, pricing power, or customer adoption lag behind the massive capex outlays.

5) Since the “Magnificent 7” (Nvidia, Microsoft, Apple, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Tesla) now represent roughly a third of the S&P 500 market capitalization, a correction would wight both on the overall market capitalization as well as on the overall US demand

Because AI leaders now make up about one-third of the U.S. stock market’s value and are responsible for around 4% of all private fixed investment, any correction in their valuations or a scaling back of their capex plans would have macro-level consequences. On the financial side, a repricing of such a large equity block would drag down overall market capitalization and household wealth, tightening financial conditions. On the real-economy side, a slowdown in $1 6 0B+ of U.S.-based annual investment would hit construction, equipment, and supplier industries, weighing directly on aggregate demand. This dual channel—financial markets and real investment—explains why an AI-driven correction would be felt far beyond the tech sector itself.
